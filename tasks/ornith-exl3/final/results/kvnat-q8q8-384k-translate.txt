## Idioms adapted and why

**1. "raining cats and dogs" → "chovendo a cântaros"**
The English version uses animals (cats and dogs) to convey heavy rain. Brazilian Portuguese has no equivalent with this imagery; instead it evokes water pouring out of large vessels (*cântaros* = big jars/pitchers). This is the culturally established idiom for a downpour.

**2. "call it a day" → "parar por hoje"**
This means to stop working for the day. Portuguese has no object-based equivalent, so the natural, everyday rendering is simply "to stop for today" (*parar por hoje*).

**3. "out of the woods" → "livres dos problemas"**
The English metaphor comes from leaving a dangerous forest for safe open ground. Portuguese doesn't use "woods" for this idea, so I translated the underlying meaning ("free from problems / out of danger"). A closer metaphorical option would be *fora do buraco*, but it's less common.

**4. "on the right track" → "no caminho certo"**
This one needed no real adaptation — the path/track metaphor transfers directly and works identically in both languages.

**5. "count our chickens before they hatch" → "contar com frango assado"**
The English idiom warns against expecting gains before they materialize. Brazilian Portuguese expresses the same idea with *não contar com frango assado* ("don't count on the roasted chicken"). Note the imagery shifts from a *live* chicken that must *hatch* to a *roasted* chicken already ready to eat — different pictures, identical meaning.

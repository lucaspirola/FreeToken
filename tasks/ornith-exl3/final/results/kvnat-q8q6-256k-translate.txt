## Idioms adapted and why

**1. "raining cats and dogs" → "chover a cântaros"**
The English image of animals falling from the sky is meaningless in Portuguese. The equivalent idiom uses the image of water pouring from jars or pitchers ("a cântaros"), which naturally conveys *heavy rain*. This is a **semantic adaptation** — the meaning is preserved, but the literal picture changes entirely.

**2. "call it a day" → "parar por hoje"**
This means *to stop working for the day*. Portuguese has no direct idiomatic equivalent, so I used a **functional/literal equivalent** ("stop for today") that sounds natural and communicates the same idea without forcing an awkward literal translation.

**3. "out of the woods" → "fora do buraco"**
The English idiom means *out of danger or difficulty*, drawing on the metaphor of leaving a forest. Portuguese uses a **different metaphor** — getting out of a hole ("fora do buraco") — to express the same sense of being out of trouble. This is a **metaphor substitution**: same meaning, different image.

**4. "on the right track" → "no caminho certo"**
This one translated almost directly. The metaphor of a *path/track* leading somewhere is shared between both languages, so it works as a **calque** (a borrowed expression that fits naturally in Portuguese).

**5. "count our chickens before they hatch" → "contar com a galinha antes que nasçam os pintos"**
This idiom means *to be premature in one's expectations*. Portuguese happens to have a nearly identical chicken-based idiom, so this was mostly a **direct translation** with only minor word-order adaptation. The shared metaphor made this one the easiest to carry over.

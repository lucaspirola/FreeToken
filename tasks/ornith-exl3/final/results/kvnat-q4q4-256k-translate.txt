## Idioms adapted and why

This English idiom means raining very heavily. Brazilian Portuguese has a different but equivalent image: *chovendo a cântaros* (literally "raining pitchers/jars"). The imagery is completely different, but the meaning is identical, so I swapped one vivid metaphor for the native one.

**2. "Call it a day" → "Encerrar por hoje"**
This means to stop working for the day. There's no direct idiomatic equivalent in Portuguese, so I used a functional one (*encerrar por hoje* / *parar por hoje*) that conveys the same intent naturally.

**3. "out of the woods" → "fora dos apuros"**
This means being past the most dangerous or difficult part. The literal "bosques" image doesn't work in Portuguese, so I adapted it to *fora dos apuros* ("out of trouble"), which carries the same sense of having escaped the worst of the situation.

**4. "on the right track" → "no caminho certo"**
This one translated almost directly. The metaphor of being on the correct path is shared across both languages, so no real adaptation was needed.

**5. "count your chickens before they hatch" → "contar com os antes de nascer"**
This means expecting a result before it actually happens. Portuguese has a parallel idiom (*não contar com os antes de nascer* / *não contar com os ovos antes de eclodirem*), so I used the native version to preserve the figurative flavor rather than translating it literally.

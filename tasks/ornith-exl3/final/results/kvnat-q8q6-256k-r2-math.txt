Let me think about the proof.

Proof that the method works:

The method relies on the principle of superposition / additivity of flows. The rate of change of water volume is the sum of the rates of all open pipes. This is because flows are additive (assuming incompressible fluid, no interaction between pipes, etc.).

Formally, let $V(t)$ be the volume of water at time $t$. Then
$$V'(t) = \sum_{i \in \text{open}(t)} r_i$$
where $\text{open}(t)$ is the set of pipes open at time $t$.

Between consecutive event times $t_k$ and $t_{k+1}$, the set of open pipes is constant, so $V'(t) = R_k$ (constant), and thus $V$ is linear on $[t_k, t_{k+1}]$ with slope $R_k$.

By continuity of $V$ (water volume can't jump), $V(t_{k+1}) = V(t_k) + R_k (t_{k+1} - t_k)$.

So we can compute $V$ at all breakpoints by summing up contributions.

To find when $V(t) = 1$: find the interval $[t_k, t_{k+1}]$ where $V(t_k) \le 1 \le V(t_{k+1})$ (assuming $V$ is increasing through 1 in that interval). Then within that interval, $V(t) = V(t_k) + R_k (t - t_k)$, so
$$1 = V(t_k) + R_k(t - t_k) \implies t = t_k + \frac{1 - V(t_k)}{R_k}.$$

This gives the exact time.

The method "always gives the right answer" because:
1. The physical model (additivity of flows) gives a piecewise linear continuous $V$.
2. The method exactly reconstructs this $V$ and solves the linear equation.
